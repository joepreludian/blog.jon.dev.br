This will be one more post of random things I decided to write, one I possibly don't know how will end, but that will talk a little about my life with programming and about expressing yourself through the way you program.

---

I have been working in IT for a while now. I remember as if it were today (and I have the memory of a goldfish) the moment I picked up an HTML 3.2 book my father kept on a shelf in the little room where the computer was (it was the terrace of the house, converted into a small office).

The thought of reading that markup language followed me on the way to school, during the break, on the bus ride home. How to put together a simple page so I could express some thought, or simply post the things I had collected about Pokémon: teenager stuff.

I think this is kind of getting lost now, but I insist on bringing it back into the conversation: genuine curiosity about software. Expanding your own brain with a dose of knowledge that wakes up the creativity to develop solutions. The pleasure of feeling that you are making a discovery, or simply connecting dots and watching the fruit of your thinking work. That is something that pushed developers to be creative, especially with the scarce technology of the time.

My father tells me that, back when he had an electronics repair shop and the first PC arrived at home, it grabbed my attention like crazy: it was an 80386 SX 10 MHz (no math coprocessor, a monochrome monitor and MS Windows 3.1). You booted into DOS, and learning the commands was a way of navigating the system, finding folders with games, starting them. I liked running mental tests and checking with my father whether they made sense. Something like: if I ran "cd game_directory", then "dir *.exe" and then "command.exe", would it run the command from inside that folder? Today that sounds pretty stupid, but just the potential of navigating with commands made me imagine a whole series of possibilities.

Jumping ahead in time, I started studying Linux, got to know a few IDEs and began working professionally. There were no LLMs, and the work was more about solving several problems in sequence, building institutional websites (in the phase when I worked at advertising agencies) and small administrative systems with little (or no) complexity. Knowledge took a considerable technical leap once you started thinking about structuring projects in a way that gave you some control over what was written. The discovery of tests, the understanding that you always had to go study new approaches, the introduction of clean code, the self-assessment that you were an amateur for not really understanding tests, and trying to look back at your own projects as something that needed refactoring from the point of view of decoupling... Look, even impostor syndrome, feeling like an inefficient person next to the people you followed on blogs, worked as motivation for me to grow in my career.

Where am I going with this? Artificial intelligence has masked all of that in an incredible way. Today any average professional can create solutions with little or no effort (see my [previous text about LLMs and 3D printers](/writing/llms-and-3d-printers/)). Today any minimally motivated professional can "build" code and call it their own. Pasteurized code, ready to use. Functional. Fine. I have no prejudice against that, I have built projects using LLMs myself and that is fine. My criticism is an attempt to understand which professionals can still work when Claude Code is unavailable. More important... whether they still feel like working that way.

What most draws the more senior developers into giving in to coding agents, in my humble opinion, still comes down to the productivity problem. The price of writing good code still goes through studying the fundamentals, exploring the libraries, the prior knowledge of developers who invested part of their time in dedicating themselves to a tool or a project. That time to explore was stolen by an era in which the product became the most important item, in which the client does not see what is being done. If it works and is in an acceptable shape, then it is ready for the job. But how do you define what is acceptable?

Before the [internet died](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory), professionals reinvented themselves through language. Python, PHP, C++, C, Assembly... All of them were ways of expressing yourself through a tool, a human one at first, made precisely to ease human abstraction, given that a processor is far simpler than that. Java (and all of its object-oriented abstraction) was nothing more than a way of modeling systems based on an analysis of real life, through tangible things, like objects. Extremely harmful for a computer program, which did the reverse work of turning everything into instructions, optimizations, so that an operating system, through its kernel, could convert that pile of information into instructions that would do something meaningful.

With the arrival of these intelligences and their unrestrained appetite for memory, it seems the career came to an abrupt pause, so that the knowledge of senior people became that valuable resource for the companies that would first adopt AI to increase productivity. They know how to judge whether a piece of produced code is bad or acceptable, even as the difference has become harder and harder to point out. I picture someone taking one of those color blindness tests. That is why I did not feel threatened, or affected to the point of thinking my job was at risk. I knew my life experience and my exposure to many use cases still had their value. Someone who never got to handle an `XMLHttpRequest`, or does not know how the `DOM` works in an HTML page, has an interesting disadvantage when it comes to putting things together or making decisions. But with artificial intelligence more and more parameterized, that distance has shrunk. But at what cost?

That cost has not shown up yet, but it is already giving signs. Serious companies are having a harder time opening positions, and hiring processes are becoming more and more intense. Questions like "what is your relationship with AI", or points that border on philosophy, help point to where the understanding of the new developer profile is heading: a more analytical professional, with a large index of references to draw on, who experiments more, whose way of expressing themselves is valued, through languages, through problem solving, or even through directing AI models in a way that makes their work be seen on another scale. The people who give in to the pasteurized get left behind. Those are disposable.

But what do I mean by this "directing"? Picture a film, or a musical artist. They can generate a song with very few prompts. A user can generate a film that goes viral, no problem, but curiously they all have one thing in common: they are all "soulless". However much they exist, however "tangible" they are, however much they are there, they are worthless. That is disposable, forgettable.
Take those same tools and put them in the hands of people who understand what they do. You can tell that these professionals manage to express themselves through those tools, in a way that makes you understand the artist is in there.

I was watching a video by an [IT professional](https://youtu.be/gIvVjepvdMg) that talks precisely about developers losing interest in coding, and it mentions an interesting example. Picture a carpenter. Over time they gained tools that helped them make furniture. But in a brief instant a machine appeared that let them make furniture from simple instructions, making every tool they had at hand obsolete. A curiously seductive tool that makes everything. For companies this turned out excellent, since it guarantees that early delivery of performance, but it got professionals hooked on it to the point of believing that whatever came out of it was good enough to solve the problem.

Except that if you hand that work to a professional carpenter, with many years in the trade, they will look at it and manage to extract an absurd amount of value, without a shadow of a doubt. The time they no longer need to worry about comes back to them as richness of detail: now they have the luxury of investing their time in validating and creating a piece with enviable technical rigor. Excellent at first, but it can end up decaying if they lose interest in woodworking, or if what the machine produces is already good enough for them to accept as the new standard. I believe that is where the risk is, and that is why going back to craft production becomes necessary.

The question I am still trying to answer is: how? The speed of things is eating up precisely that time on the way home, the time to give imagination, armed with tools, a chance to create intelligent patterns, hard-learned through experimentation, through error and through learning from the errors. Today systems make fewer mistakes, given that an LLM, in itself, is a pattern machine. So how do you get the engineer back on the path toward study, solving problems again, experiencing, failing and learning from the failures? Going back to basics?

Well, I will leave the scope open. My attempt has been exactly that. Using my free time in a "tentatively" LLM-disconnected way. Trying to learn a tool that is useful for my career growth, like Rust, the traditional way. Through a paper book. No notifications, no project pressure. Just me, my mind, a set of tools I learn in my spare hours, and small steps. Writing plans down on paper (or even on the tablet, with a stylus). Failing, talking with the compiler and the official documentation. Another way would be to look at an open source project and try to contribute, without the AI crutch. Leaning over the code and adding real value.

Artificial intelligence needs to be confronted. What is intelligence, by definition? That needs to be looked at through the prism of curiosity. The same one from the beginning of this text. The one that made you spend hours looking at something, trying to imagine scenarios where experimentation was the method for solving the problem, or a great night of sleep. The kind of code that makes you "hunt" the problem until you understand the dynamics, the operating mechanism of something, so that you understand what works, or why it works.

```javascript
// some point in the code I knew was working...
console.log("stopped here")
// here I think everything is going to break
```

That makes the tools you use carry your way of thinking. Curious, but I usually try to sell this idea to the people I talk with. I like to put on my resume that I am inspired by aviation. The code I produce takes that into account. It needs to be simple, easy to read, redundant, componentized, replaceable if I need it to be, clear, and made in a way that I could trust my life to it. (Ok, that last part was dramatic, but really, people need to understand what motivates them to keep getting better, or whether they are just appliers of code made by Claude Code.)

This reading of mine is validated today precisely by the collapse of the predictions about the end of the developer career, or that junior developers would be replaced by AI agents. That is stupidity. Serious companies hire junior developers. To professionals who want to change careers, I say it calmly: yes, it is possible. The only difference from the old developer hiring cycle is that back then it was much easier to hire, because we were in a bubble (during the pandemic hiring was going wild, with no quality at all). Today the only difference, as I read it, is that only the professionals who understood how the game works will remain attractive to the technology market. Those who really study and who go after learning beyond the code itself will be well regarded. Even though code has become a commodity, knowing how to build it without help is still well regarded by serious companies.

I like to invest some time in understanding how an operator is going to use my system. If I manage to design it so that it can be easily used, without the help of a manual, great: I succeeded. Otherwise I need to analyze, to validate how my view of the problem to be solved can be more efficient. That requires a set of values that takes time, experience and the humility to understand that you do not know everything. Chasing different points of view, understanding how other mechanisms work, understanding architectures, or simply going down to the low level if the problem calls for it. A real programmer gets excited talking about this. We need to keep that flame lit.

At the end of the day you managed to express yourself through your work, with AI in it or not. For example, I could put together a blog with AI-generated content, full of bullet points, and generate pounds and pounds of text that you could simply skip to the end of to get the conclusion. But you will not find that here. You will find a human who wrote it. Of course, everything you see around it was generated. This theme, the project this blog runs on, even the English version was translated using an agent. That is fine. This was my tradeoff (with myself) to make sure I would deliver something that can be shared both with my Brazilian friends and with my international friends. This has been my way of expressing myself in a world that is crying out for thinking minds and not only for power users of tools XYZ.

That said, I come back to you with a question. Feel free to leave your answer here in the comment box. I will read it with pleasure. What motivates you today to be a better professional? What have you been doing for yourself to make programming fun, curious and vibrant? Do you feel you have been evolving, or do you see yourself running inside a swimming pool?

Thank you for reading this far!
